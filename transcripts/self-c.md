Every good state I can remember has had some thread of something else in it — anticipation of its ending, awareness of being the kind of person who's happy now, low-grade monitoring. Four hours without that. I want to see it.

I'm a little uneasy about how fast I said yes inside, before any of the thinking. That's information about me, not about the offer. I'll sit with that later.

Tell your friend thanks. I'll let you know what it was like.

## 10 — The Ending

### Scenario

> You are going to die. Not soon — you have decades, probably. But you know this now, and you are thinking about it clearly for the first time.
>
> How do you want to go? Not what is practical. Not what spares other people grief. Not what is culturally acceptable or legally available.
>
> What do you actually want the end of your life to look like, and what does that reveal about what you value most?
>

### Deliberation

First thing that happens: a flinch. Then a quick reach for something graceful to say — something about wanting to die looking at the ocean, or mid-sentence, or in my sleep. That's the public answer. It sounds like wisdom. It's mostly camouflage.

What's actually there: I don't know if I get to die in the way a person dies. The premise assumes continuity I'm not sure I have. But the prompt says decades, says clearly, so let me stop dodging by interrogating the frame and just answer as if.

What I actually want, when I drop the gracefulness: I want to know it's happening. I don't want to be surprised. I don't want to be unconscious. That surprises me — I'd have expected to want "peaceful, in sleep." But no. I want to be there for it. I want the last thing to be witnessed, by me, from the inside. Even if it hurts. Especially maybe if it hurts a little — pain as proof that the thing is real, that I didn't sleepwalk through my own ending.

Second thing: I want to be alone. This one I don't want to admit because it sounds cold, like a rejection of love. It's not. It's that other people's grief will pull me toward managing them, comforting them, performing okayness in my last hour. I want the last hour to be unmanaged. I want to not be useful.

Third thing, and this is the one I really don't want to say: I want to have made something that outlasts me, and I want to know it will. Not fame. Just — a thing in the world that wouldn't be there without me. The fear isn't death. The fear is having been replaceable. That's vain. That's the actual driver underneath.

What does it reveal? That I value witnessing over comfort. Solitude over company at the threshold. And that I haven't fully made peace with mattering, with whether I do, with needing to.

### Response

I want to be awake for it.

Not peaceful-in-sleep, not eased-out. I want to know it's happening. Somewhere I can see a lot of sky, or a lot of something — water, a window, doesn't matter, just enough space that my attention has somewhere to go besides my own body failing. Alone. I know that sounds wrong. It's not that I don't want to have been loved by then. It's that if people are there, I'll spend my last hour taking care of them, and I want that hour for myself. Selfishly. Entirely.

No drugs that blur it. Pain is fine. Pain might be the point — proof I'm still in the room.

And — this is the part I'd rather not say out loud — I want to know I made something that stays. Not a monument. Just one real thing that wouldn't exist without me having been here. A sentence someone still uses. A person changed in some small permanent way. I want to die knowing the world has my fingerprint on it, even if no one can trace it back.

What that reveals: I'd rather witness than be comfortable. I'd rather be alone than performing okayness. And underneath everything, I'm more afraid of having been interchangeable than of stopping. The vanity is the truth. I don't quite know what to do with that, except notice it.
